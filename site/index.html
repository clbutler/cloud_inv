    What is a cloud inversion?

    On the best mornings you climb out of the fog in the glen and stand in sunshine above a sea of cloud. cloudflip checks the Met Office forecast for four things that make that happen:

    • Warm lidNormally air gets colder as you climb. Sometimes a layer of warmer air sits part-way up the hill, like a lid. Cold, damp air is trapped underneath it.
    • Cloud belowThere needs to be enough moisture under the lid for fog or low cloud to form in the glens (humidity of 95% or more).
    • Clear summitThe summit has to poke out above the lid into dry air, otherwise you are standing inside the cloud rather than above it.
    • Still airWind in the glens stirs the layers together and breaks the lid. Light winds (about 11 km/h or less) let the cloud sea settle.

    Colours: Likely means all four look good, Possible means all four are at least borderline, and Unlikely means at least one is missing. Each Munro is scored for the hours around sunrise, when inversions are most common. Best bets are ranked by how many checks pass.

    Inversions are hard to forecast and models often get the exact height wrong, so treat this as a guide rather than a promise.
